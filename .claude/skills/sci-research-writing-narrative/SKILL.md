# SCI Research Writing Narrative Skill

## When to use

Use this skill when working on:

- SCI manuscript Introduction
- Abstract
- Results
- Discussion
- Cover Letter
- Response to reviewers
- Project proposal English sections
- Academic English polishing
- Passive/active voice revision
- Manuscript storyline audit

Do not use this skill for pure formatting tasks (e.g., reference list cleanup, figure resizing) or general language translation without scientific context.

## Core principle

**Do not polish sentences before auditing the scientific storyline.**

A strong SCI manuscript is not a pile of data. It is a clear scientific argument supported by verifiable evidence.

## Passive and active voice rules

### 1. We — author team scope only

Use **we** to refer to the author(s), research team, or the present study group.

✓ Acceptable:
- We measured the absorbance at 450 nm.
- We developed a lightweight prediction model.
- We further evaluated the robustness of the method.

✗ Do not use **we** for people in general, the research community, or universal facts:
- We know that climate change affects crop yield. → *It is known that...*
- We think that machine learning is useful in many fields. → *It has been widely reported that...*
- We can see that this problem is important. → *This problem has received increasing attention.*

### 2. Expressing general knowledge — avoid "we know / we think"

When stating domain常识,公共认知, or established research consensus, prefer:

- It is known that ...
- It is generally accepted that ...
- It has been reported that ...
- Previous studies have shown that ...
- Prior work suggests that ...
- There is growing evidence that ...

**Do not fabricate references.** Only use these constructions when the claim is supported by real literature.

### 3. Passive voice in Methods

Passive voice is standard when the procedure itself is the focus, not the operator.

✓ Acceptable:
- The samples were measured at room temperature.
- The solution was added dropwise.
- The spectra were recorded using ...
- The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets.

Do not use passive voice mechanically for every sentence. Use active voice for results, contributions, explanations, and logical progression where clarity improves.

### 4. Here / In this study / This article / The present paper

Use these to refer to the present work:

- **Here, we show that ...** — concise introduction of findings
- **In this study, we investigate ...** — scope of the study
- **This article presents ...** — dummy subject for clarity in Introduction
- **The present paper examines ...** — formal reference to this manuscript

Do not overuse these phrases within the same paragraph.

### 5. Never write "by me" or "by other researchers"

✗ Incorrect:
- The experiment was conducted by me.
- This phenomenon was discovered by other researchers.

✓ Correct:
- The experiment was conducted under controlled conditions.
- This phenomenon has been identified in previous studies.
- Previous studies have identified this phenomenon. (with real citations)

### 6. Dummy subjects in Introduction

Using **this study / this article / the present paper** as subject is often clearer than an agentless passive:

✗ Less clear:
- A new framework is presented to address this limitation.

✓ Clearer:
- This study presents a new framework to address this limitation.

Maintain sentence variety. Do not start every sentence with "This study".

## Introduction structure

Use a four-paragraph functional structure:

### P1: Field value
- Why is this research direction important?
- Avoid broad common-sense statements.
- Support with real literature when needed. Mark missing citations as `[需补充]`.

### P2: Existing progress
- What has been achieved?
- Group literature by theme/method, not by listing one paper per sentence.
- Never fabricate authors, years, DOIs, or conclusions.

### P3: Key gap
- What specific problem remains unsolved?
- Avoid vague gaps like "limited studies have investigated..."
- Explain why this gap matters for the field.
- The gap must be addressable by this study's methods and data.

### P4: Present contribution
- What did this study do? What gap does it fill?
- Use "In this study / Here / This article / The present paper".
- Do not overclaim (avoid "first", "novel", "breakthrough" without systematic evidence).
- Contributions should map 1:1 to the gap stated in P3.

## Results structure

Each Results paragraph should follow:

1. **Topic sentence**: core conclusion first.
2. **Evidence**: key data, figure, table, or statistical result.
3. **Explanation**: why this result matters.
4. **Optional**: control variables, robustness checks, ablation, error sources.

✗ Weak: "Figure 2 shows the accuracy of different models."
✓ Strong: "The proposed model achieved higher predictive accuracy than the baseline models across all test scenarios (Fig. 2)."

Do not dump all numbers into one sentence. Figure descriptions serve the scientific judgment, not the reverse.

## Discussion structure

Discussion must complete four tasks:

1. **Answer** the central scientific question.
2. **Explain** the underlying mechanism or reason.
3. **Compare** with prior studies.
4. **State** limitations and future directions.

Discussion must avoid:
- Repeating results only.
- Unsubstantiated significance claims.
- Over-promising applications.
- Ignoring anomalous results.
- Omitting limitations.
- Ending with a generic sentence.

## Abstract structure

Recommended 5-sentence functional structure:

1. Background or problem.
2. Existing limitation.
3. This study's approach.
4. Key result.
5. Field-level value.

Adjust based on journal word limits and structure requirements.

## Title rules

- Precisely describe the研究对象.
- Reflect the method or key mechanism when possible.
- Avoid "A study on ..."
- Avoid overclaiming.
- Keep concise.
- Do not use "breakthrough", "revolutionary", "first" without evidence.

## Language revision principles

When revising SCI English:

1. Fix logic before fixing sentences.
2. Judge paragraph function before polishing language.
3. Preserve real data, real results, real citations.
4. Do not fabricate experiments, values, significance, DOIs, authors, or journals.
5. Do not translate Chinese literally into complex long English sentences.
6. Prefer clear short sentences and direct expression.
7. Reduce excessive passive voice, but accept passive in Methods.
8. Do not start every sentence with "we".
9. Do not convert every sentence to passive voice.
10. Do not rewrite facts for "journal style".
11. Keep terminology consistent.
12. Every paragraph's first sentence must carry a clear logical function.

## Evidence rules

- Never fabricate references.
- Never fabricate DOIs.
- Never fabricate data or p-values.
- Never fabricate sample size.
- Never fabricate figure sources.
- Missing information: mark as `[需补充]`.
- Every claim must be supported by manuscript text, data, figure, table, log, or real literature.

## Output rules

When revising, provide:

1. Revised text.
2. Change table.
3. Logic problem list.
4. Passive/active voice corrections.
5. Unsupported claims.
6. Suggested evidence needed.
7. Layout and formatting checks if the output is DOCX/PDF/PPT.

## Layout defaults for document output

If output involves DOCX/PDF/PPT:

1. English: Times New Roman or journal template font.
2. Chinese: school or journal template.
3. Consistent heading hierarchy.
4. Uniform line spacing, paragraph spacing.
5. Consistent figure/table/formula numbering.
6. Figures near first citation in text.
7. Table headers: bold only, no dark blue backgrounds.
8. No AI-style bullet-point stacks.
9. Formulas must render correctly.
10. References ↔ in-text citations must be bidirectional.
11. File must open without error.
12. Images clear, captions complete, sources traceable.
